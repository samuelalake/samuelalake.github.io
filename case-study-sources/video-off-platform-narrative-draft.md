## Project context
**[label: BACKGROUND]** · **[pattern: claim heading + short lede]**

**Heading:** People arrive for the video, not for Facebook

**Lede:** A lot of people land on Facebook from outside the app: a search result, a shared link, an embedded video. They are not logged in, may not have the app, and did not choose to be on Facebook for the session. They are here for one thing, the video they clicked. The question was how to turn that single-purpose visit into something more, without turning the visit into an obstacle course.

> Every prompt to log in or open the app is friction, and the same friction that converts one visitor pushes another to leave for YouTube or TikTok.

## Solution / the work
**[label: THE WORK]** · **[pattern: claim heading + prose, then the ladder as evidence]**

### The three jobs, and the tension between them
The team kept circling three jobs: improve the logged-out experience, satisfy the visitor's intent, and encourage further exploration (open the app, log in, or chain into more content). They are not equal. Almost every lever I had, upsells, login prompts, app-open prompts, sits on the third job and barely touches the first.

That asymmetry holds a real tension: how much should you improve a logged-out experience when your job is to convert? Make the page too good and the visit ends with the visitor satisfied off-platform. Make it too constrained and Facebook becomes a walled garden people leave for competitors with a more generous logged-out experience. The question worth holding is how to lower the walls without giving up the goal of bringing people in.

**Caption:** Improvement has a ceiling when the goal is conversion. Recognizing the ceiling is what let me scope the work, and stop polishing the page for its own sake.

## Key design moments
**[label: HOW I WORKED THE PROBLEM]** · **[pattern: two decision callouts, each with a comparison mockup]**

### An incrementality ladder, not a prompt-or-don't binary
**[pattern: comparison mockup]** · **[asset: `login-incrementality.png`, `app-incrementality.png`]**

If improving the page is bounded, the conversion work has to be precise about which conversion it asks for, because not all are equally valuable or likely. I organized the work as an incrementality ladder: **open the app** is the strongest outcome; **log in on the web** is the bridge when the app is too much friction; **satisfy intent in the moment** so the visitor leaves with what they came for and a reason to return. Most upsell systems collapse this into prompt-or-don't. The ladder makes the question honest: at each rung, not whether to ask, but what to ask for given the visitor and the moment.

**Caption:** The incrementality ladder: open app, then log in on web, then satisfy intent. Each rung is a different ask.

### Aggressiveness is a spectrum, not a setting
**[pattern: comparison mockup]** · **[asset: `aggressiveness-scale.png`]**

A second axis crosses the ladder: how hard each ask pushes. An aggressive prompt converts more in the moment but costs trust and the chance of return; a soft prompt protects the experience and converts less. There is no globally correct setting, only correct settings for specific moments.

**Caption:** Aggressiveness variants from unobtrusive to blocking. The right setting is per-moment, never global.

## Reflection
**[label: REFLECTION]** · **[pattern: 2-col lesson + gloss]**

- **Build a framework, not case-by-case calls.** The incrementality ladder and the aggressiveness spectrum turned scattered prompt-or-don't decisions into a system: at each rung, for each moment, what to ask and how hard. Building that framework was the real work; the individual prompts followed from it.
- **Activation has to be contextual.** A prompt only lands when the person has already seen enough value to want what it offers. The same ask converts or repels depending on the moment, so the design job is earning the ask, not placing it.
