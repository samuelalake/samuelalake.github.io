A social cookbook built from recipes you find everywhere

The problem

Recipes live everywhere, and nowhere you can cook from

A recipe found in a social post or blog can disappear, change, or remain awkward to use in the kitchen. Bookmarking keeps the link, but it does not make the recipe easier to plan, shop, or cook. Trove keeps recipes in one place so you can return when you are ready to use them.

The product

From something you find to something you can cook

Trove connects the whole recipe journey: import something you want to make, discover more through other people, plan it into the week, cook it, and shop for what comes next.

02 · Import

Bring recipes in from wherever you find them

From Safari, someone can share a recipe directly to Trove without leaving the browsing flow. Inside the app, they can also tap Add and paste a link. Both paths create a private, editable copy for that person.

03 · Discover

Find recipes through people

Search returns people and recipes together, keeping the person behind a recommendation visible alongside the dishes they share.

04 · Plan and cook

Plan the week, then cook one step at a time

Recipes from a person's Trove can be placed into a weekly plan. Guided steps and timers then make each recipe easier to follow in the kitchen.

05 · Shop

Turn several recipes into one shopping list

Trove gives you one list to maintain across the week. Someone can move an item from To buy to Stocked as their pantry changes, then hand the remaining list to a retailer.

Product decisions

Organize recipes for scanning

Friends told me Discover was not showing enough food that felt familiar or reflected their nationality. The flat feed already ranked recipes using saved preferences, but ranking alone did not make the range easy to scan. I regrouped Discover by cuisine, gave each cuisine a dedicated page, and added a star so people could explicitly shape what appears more often.

Use the retailer path that works today

The grocery list needed a useful next step, but retailer support was uneven. Kroger supports sending items directly to a cart. Amazon's cart API requires ongoing sales volume and is being deprecated, so I used an affiliate handoff for launch instead of letting a deeper integration block V1.

Give each person a recipe they can make their own

Recipe publishers depend on visits to the original page, and copyright means Trove cannot republish their directions as public content. Trove therefore keeps the publicly visible recipe limited to its cover, ingredients, attribution, and source link.

When someone wants to cook, Trove opens the publisher's page and imports the directions into a private copy owned by that user. The publisher receives the visit, while the person can edit, plan, and cook from their copy without exposing those changes publicly.

How might we make saving and sharing recipes feel social?

Keeping each person's working copy private raised a second question: how could Trove still make recipe discovery feel connected to other people? V1 includes attribution, public profiles, People search, following, and saves, so someone can see who shared a recipe and find more from them. With more users, I want to test community suggestions, notes, and recipe remixing without exposing personal edits.

Where it stands

TestFlight begins the next learning loop

Trove is available on TestFlight with the core journey working from discovery and import through planning, cooking, and grocery.

The next phase is to market the app, bring in more users, and collect enough product data to decide what deserves to grow. I am particularly interested in community suggestions and notes, retailer coverage in more countries, and whether existing areas of the app should expand into meal journaling or calorie tracking.

Reflection

Choosing what matters most

Pivoting and scoping came from the same discipline: choose the problem with the clearest product and business value, then prioritize the smallest version you can build and ship.

Know when and how to pivot

The project began closer to health and behavior change. I narrowed toward the problem with a clearer flywheel and more immediate value: helping recipes move from discovery into planning, shopping, and cooking.

Scope, scope, and scope

Once the problem was clear, I drew the V1 boundary around importing a cookable copy and making it useful across the week. Richer commerce and community contribution could follow after the core product was reliable.
