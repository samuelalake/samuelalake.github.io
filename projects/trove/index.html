A social cookbook built from recipes you find everywhere

The problem

Recipes live everywhere, and nowhere you can cook from

A recipe found in a social post or blog can disappear, change, or remain awkward to use in the kitchen. Saving the link does not make the recipe easier to plan, shop, or cook. Trove becomes your treasure trove: one place to keep recipes and return when you are ready to complete the journey.

The product

From something you find to something you can cook

Trove turns scattered recipes into a repeatable flywheel: discover something worth cooking, add it to your Trove, plan it into the week, cook it, and shop for what comes next.

01 · Onboarding

Onboarding introduces the full loop—from finding and importing recipes to planning, cooking, and shopping—before someone enters the product.

02 · Discover

Find recipes through people and the open web

Search returns people and recipes together, keeping the cooks behind a recommendation visible alongside dishes from the open web.

Two import paths

03 · Import

Bring recipes in from wherever you find them

From Safari, someone can share a recipe directly to Trove without leaving the browsing flow. Inside the app, they can also tap Add and paste a link. Both paths create a private, editable copy.

04 · Plan and cook

Plan the week, then cook one step at a time

Recipes from your Trove can be placed into a weekly plan. Guided steps and timers turn the saved recipe into something easier to follow in the kitchen.

05 · Shop

Turn several recipes into one shopping list

Trove gives you one list to maintain across the week. Someone can move an item from To buy to Stocked as their pantry changes, then hand the remaining list to a retailer.

Product decisions

Give each person a private copy they can cook from

Bookmarking saves a link to someone else's recipe, but it does not give the person a version they can edit, plan, or cook from if the source changes. Trove keeps the public recipe connected to its publisher, then creates a private copy for each person when they import it.

Keep the recipe connected to its source

Public recipe cards show the cover, ingredients, who saved it, and the original publisher, but not the directions because Trove cannot republish them. When someone wants to cook, Trove opens the source and imports the directions into their own copy. The publisher gets the visit, while the person's edits, plans, and cooking history stay private to them.

Building for social connection

How might we make saving and sharing recipes feel social?

When each person owns a private copy, social behavior has to happen around it rather than inside it. Attribution, profiles, People search, following, and saves show where a recipe came from without exposing someone's personal edits. As the community grows, suggestions or remixing could let people contribute back without turning the private copy into a shared document.

Organize recipes for scanning and preference-setting

The flat feed already ranked recipes using saved preferences, but it made cuisines difficult to scan. I reorganized Discover into cuisine sections, each with a dedicated page. A star in the page header lets people explicitly tell Trove which cuisines they want to see more often.

Where it stands

Available on TestFlight

The end-to-end product is available to test while I prepare for a broader launch. The current loop runs from discovery and import through planning, cooking, and grocery.

Reflection

Choosing what matters most

Pivoting and scoping came from the same discipline: choose the problem with the clearest product and business value, then prioritize the smallest version you can build and ship.

Know when and how to pivot

The project began closer to health and behavior change. I narrowed toward the problem with a clearer flywheel and more immediate value: helping recipes move from discovery into planning, shopping, and cooking.

Scope, scope, and scope

Once the problem was clear, I drew the V1 boundary around importing a cookable copy and making it useful across the week. Richer commerce and community contribution could follow after the core product was reliable.
