Rem · Live on the App Store · 2025

Turning everyday context into self-executing AI tasks

Rem is a personal AI assistant that captures intent, clarifies it into work, and runs approved actions through a cloud or Mac gateway the user controls.

Lead Product Designer & Engineer

Overview

How might an assistant turn everyday intent into protected time, and real progress?

Rem started from my own workflow. I could capture what I meant to do, but turning intentions into protected time was the hard part, and I spent more time managing tools than doing the work.

Product direction

Initiated the idea, brought collaborators in, and set the strategy across the capture-to-action loop.

Interaction design

Designed how the assistant serves multiple jobs and communicates its status clearly.

Implementation

Built the core app experience in SwiftUI, moving between design and engineering.

Problem

Today, work is split across apps that capture but never follow through

A task app captures intent, a calendar protects time, a notes app holds context. AI chat can summarize or rewrite, but it rarely moves work forward in a way you can trust. People manage more tools instead of finishing more work.

Early exploration

Opportunity

Voice to capture, a controlled runtime as the moat

Productivity is a huge category, and people now expect to get things done by talking to software. But that adoption has produced better conversation, not better follow-through.

One loop

Connect the surfaces people already use into a single capture-clarify-act loop.

Voice-first

Voice as the entry point, so capture feels closer to thinking out loud than filing into an app.

Scoped execution

Actions run through a gateway the user owns, and can be reviewed or revoked.

Capture & clarify

Finding the balance between assistant-led and user-led

We studied voice and AI products to understand who leads the interaction and how much structure to show. HUXE leads the conversation and packages context; Todoist Ramble lets the user lead with loose input. Rem needed the balance between them.

The execution layer

Not another place to talk to AI, but a runtime where it can act

Acting on intent was always the goal, but for a long time it was the most ambiguous part of the loop. As the landscape shifted it got easier to name: OpenClaw showed a remote-control model, Claude made approved computer actions feel mainstream, and Codex made the control surface concrete on mobile.

OpenClaw works like a remote control: a remote issues commands, and a runtime carries them out on a connected device. That raised the central question, what device runs the actions and where does it live. Connecting to a user's own Mac was the obvious answer, but for an iOS-first product I made a deliberate call to start with a cloud gateway, which derisked the harder problems before committing to native Mac work.

Then the challenge was trust: simplify a powerful runtime into something a normal person could rely on. Fewer pairing steps, and status that stays legible, healthy, loading, or failing, with a clear path to recovery. Settings became an elevated part of the experience because it had to carry status, permissions, and capabilities.

Where it is now

Shipped, found the retention gap, relaunching

The first release did its job as an experiment. It validated the planning-assistant surface, but reliability gaps and a missing new-user experience kept people from understanding why to return. Usage data made the problem visible: the first version did not yet create enough daily return behavior.

The relaunch focuses on exactly that, a clearer first run, a more reliable gateway connection, and a simpler explanation of what Rem can do. In parallel we are building the Mac app as the user-owned runtime, where execution feels local, inspectable, and under the user's control.

Reflection

What I learned

The model is only one part of the work.

The product still decides what the user is trying to do, what context the assistant can use, what action it is allowed to take, and how the user stays in control.

Useful AI work needs structure around it.

The work has to be scoped, reviewed, tested, and tied to a real outcome, or it becomes another source of ambiguity.
