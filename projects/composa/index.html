The problem

Moving a design into video usually means starting over

Two things break when designers move into video. Their Figma or slide-making skills no longer map to the editor, and the design itself becomes a flat asset without its original layers or controls.

The product

If you can use Figma or make slides, you can make video

Composa keeps the canvas and property controls designers already know, then adds the timelines and media tools needed for video.

01 · Compose

Design on a canvas you know

Paste a design from Figma, then arrange its layers with the same canvas controls, layout models, and property panel designers expect from a design tool.

02 · Add media

Put video and audio on one timeline

Import video and audio, then place them on separate tracks under one playhead.

03 · Animate

Animate like it’s Keynote

Start with Build in, Action, and Build out, then use the motion gizmo, keyframe diamonds, easing, and property controls to refine the result.

04 · Direct

Use AI as an assistant editor

Describe a change and Composa applies it to the canvas or timeline. The result stays editable.

Key decisions for V1

What makes V1 lovable?

The first version was centered on one flow: paste a design from Figma, add video and audio, animate it, and export one video.

Keep the design mental model

The canvas, layers, property controls, and motion presets should feel familiar before someone learns anything new.

Prove one complete loop

Figma-to-video parity is the direction. V1 earns it by making the design, media, motion, and export loop coherent end to end.

Competitive research

Identify opportunities beyond the basics

I studied design, slide, video, audio, and AI tools to understand the wider market. The research surfaced emerging behaviors and jobs Composa could support after proving its first editing loop.

Designing media for V1

A video editor needs more than a timeline

The canvas and timeline established the editing model, but a credible video editor also needs media and audio effects. I used Sequence as a reference and translated its controls into Composa’s design system.

Media effects

Translate the useful controls from the reference into a system that belongs beside Composa’s canvas properties.

Audio effects

Give audio the same legible, direct-manipulation model as the rest of the editor.

Where it stands

Shipped and available in public beta

Composa is live and available to use now. I am tracking how designers use the public beta as I decide whether to keep growing it as a side project or turn it into an open-core, founder-led business.

Reflection

What I learned

Building Composa taught me how to work with AI at two levels: inside my own design and engineering workflow, and across a repeatable process for turning product decisions into verified software. The second required designing how agents receive work, check their results, and bring decisions back to me.

Make AI part of the design loop

I used AI to move between interface references, specifications, and working code. Reviewing the result in the product helped me refine the next instruction. Clear design intent and concrete feedback mattered as much as the prompt.

Design the system that builds the product

Beyond individual AI-assisted tasks, I built a repeatable workflow around scoped agent work, shared specifications, verification, and a human review-and-merge gate. My role expanded to defining what agents could decide, what evidence counted as done, and where I needed to step in. That process let me direct a wider range of work while keeping responsibility for the result.
